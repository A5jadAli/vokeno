# English unit map and pilot specification

Version 1, 27 October 2026. Read with the [curriculum plan](english-curriculum-plan.md). This is the full authoring sequence. A1.01 to A1.03 now have an implemented 12-session pilot; the remaining rows are plans, not published lessons. See the [review packet](english-a1-pilot-review.md). It replaces the earlier review's provisional three-unit outline.

## How to use this map

There are 92 proposed core units: A1 24, A2 20, B1 20, B2 16 and C1 12. There are also 18 professional missions that apply core skills to meetings, interviews and presentations. Missions do not count as additional CEFR levels or require a separate duplicate language course.

`A1.01` is the planning shorthand for unit ID `en-a1-u1`; its pilot session IDs are `en-a1-u1-meet`, `en-a1-u1-build`, `en-a1-u1-listen` and `en-a1-u1-use`. Apply the same convention to Units 2 and 3. Preserve existing legacy `en-*` IDs separately. Level labels and grammar placement below are proposed curriculum decisions that require teacher validation, not official CEFR prescriptions.

The default prerequisite of each core unit is the previous unit's relevant capabilities, including earlier foundations. Diagnostic evidence may cover these prerequisites. A four-unit block ends with a cumulative checkpoint using new details and a parallel retake. Checkpoints recommend support without locking browsing. A1 therefore has six blocks, A2 and B1 five each, B2 four and C1 three. Final-block checkpoints also sample earlier blocks.

Every unit includes listening and reading input plus spoken and written use. The last column identifies a culminating task, not the only activity in the unit. Writing grows from labels/forms/messages to sustained texts; listening and reading grow from short supported input to extended unfamiliar material. Each block also revisits familiar language for fluency and includes reviewed American and British voices.

The core teaches general communicative capabilities. Where a row uses a professional example, everyday learners receive an equivalent social, community or study situation with the same language demand. Professional missions then apply those capabilities to specific roles. Exam technique never enters the default sequence.

## Detailed specification for the first three units

### Shared pilot rules

- Twelve short sessions in total, four per unit. Target about five to eight minutes per session; measure with beginners and split if necessary. These are design estimates, not a deadline or a promise of mastery.
- Keep new review targets to manageable teaching segments, initially around four to seven, while checking total task load. A phrase list's length alone does not measure difficulty.
- Each session retrieves earlier language. Numbers, alphabet, dates, payment and sophisticated politeness explanations must not enter a marked task before they have been taught.
- Use original fictional people and clear scenarios. American and British models share the objective; word choice changes only where naturalness requires it.
- Provide at least one reviewed narrator and one distinct scene speaker for each variety across the pilot. These are production requirements, not claims that four speakers is an experimentally optimal number.
- Offer self-practice without a microphone. Keep its completion separate from observed speech. Live practice and any saved sample require the normal microphone flow.
- A scored bounded question has a determinate answer. An open personal answer has a task rubric and labelled feedback, not an undisclosed finite list of approved sentences.
- No em dashes in newly authored learner-facing copy or these lesson scripts. Naturalness must come from good language and context, not punctuation tricks.

### Unit 1: Meet someone and ask for repetition

**Outcome:** greet someone, exchange names, ask for repetition and close the exchange. **Prerequisites:** none; optional first-use demonstration. **Scene:** two people arriving at a class or community activity.

Initial models: `Hello.`, `I'm Alex.`, `What's your name?`, `Nice to meet you.`, `Please say that again.`, `Thanks. Bye.` Teach `Hi` as a valid familiar alternative, without requiring a second memorised greeting card. Introduce `you` and the practical meaning of the name question through the exchange rather than a pronoun lecture.

| Session           | Teach and practise                                                                                             | Output and marking                                                                                                      |
| ----------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `en-a1-u1-meet`   | Demonstrate play/replay, hear a short introduction, match a spoken name to a badge; teach greeting/name chunks | Choose the named person from a small set; no reading-heavy explanation required                                         |
| `en-a1-u1-build`  | Model `I'm + name` and `What's your name?`; practise question/answer order                                     | Build a supplied sentence. A typing check can complete a supplied fictional name; personal-name production remains open |
| `en-a1-u1-listen` | Teach repetition and a friendly close; hear familiar language from another speaker                             | Ask for repetition when the name is unclear, then identify the repeated name                                            |
| `en-a1-u1-use`    | Combine the exchange, first with cues and then with fewer cues                                                 | Say a name, ask the other person's name and close. Write a simple introduction. Record support use and offer one retry  |

**Pronunciation:** make the name audible and group `I'm Alex` naturally; use a clear question model. Do not teach IPA, require a specific British/American sound or claim that every question rises.

**Changed version:** use a different partner/name. **Delayed use:** Unit 2 begins with an introduction; a later review asks the learner to begin without the original model. **Rubric:** communication of a name, appropriate response, and use of repair when needed. Being able to spell the name is not required here.

**Bounded-answer example:** badge says `Alex`; prompt says `Complete: I'm ___. Use the name on the badge.` Accept `Alex` under normal punctuation/capitalisation tolerance. Do not use this answer list to grade `Introduce yourself`, which permits other names and formulations.

### Unit 2: Tell someone about yourself

**Outcome:** exchange simple information about origin, home and work/study. **Prerequisites:** A1.01 introduction and repetition. **Scene:** continue the conversation with a new classmate or neighbour/neighbor.

Teach `Where are you from?`, `I'm from ...`, `Where do you live?`, `I live in ...`, plus a small choice of work/study models such as `I'm a student` and `I work in a shop`. Treat these as useful patterns first. Supply only the place/role vocabulary needed by each scene, with meaning support. Do not require a memorised list of nationalities.

| Session           | Teach and practise                                                                                | Output and marking                                                                                                      |
| ----------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `en-a1-u2-meet`   | Retrieve name language; distinguish origin from current home using a short profile and scene      | Match each place to the correct fact; explicitly teach the distinction before testing it                                |
| `en-a1-u2-build`  | Build `I'm from` and `I live in` replies; introduce work/study models in a separate short segment | Complete supplied profile facts; judge open personal facts by task relevance, not whether they match fictional examples |
| `en-a1-u2-listen` | Hear two short profiles, one American and one British, using familiar language                    | Identify who lives where and use repetition to confirm one unclear detail                                               |
| `en-a1-u2-use`    | Exchange name, origin and home/role with prompts fading                                           | Write a brief introduction and answer a partner's question with different details                                       |

**Pronunciation:** clarity of the key place/role word and meaningful phrase grouping. Avoid diagnosing errors from a text transcript. **Changed version:** the person comes from one place and now lives in another. **Delayed use:** introduce the person to someone else later, with a supplied profile when third-person grammar has not yet been taught.

**Rubric:** the listener can identify the relevant facts and the learner can distinguish the two place questions. Learners may use fictional information. A longer correct answer is not an error simply because the model is short.

### Unit 3: Order something and confirm it

**Outcome:** request one drink, understand a service choice and correct a misheard item. **Prerequisites:** A1.01 politeness/repair and A1.02 short question/answer participation. **Scene:** a simple café order; payment is outside this unit.

Teach a small drink set and `I'd like a coffee, please`, `Yes, please`, `No, thank you`, and a short correction such as `No, tea, please`. Introduce the here/takeaway distinction as a separate teaching segment. Model `To take away, please` in the British version and `To go, please` in the American version; accept either when the intended request is clear.

| Session           | Teach and practise                                                               | Output and marking                                                                         |
| ----------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| `en-a1-u3-meet`   | Read a tiny menu with meaning support; hear a request; teach the request pattern | Match the requested drink and build one polite request                                     |
| `en-a1-u3-build`  | Change the requested item; practise acceptance/refusal and a simple correction   | Respond meaningfully to a known option; do not require one exact full sentence             |
| `en-a1-u3-listen` | Teach the service question and choice before hearing both regional models        | Choose here/takeaway correctly from audio and notice a confirmation with the wrong drink   |
| `en-a1-u3-use`    | Complete an order with one confirmation turn                                     | Correct the misheard item if needed and write a one-line order note using supplied details |

**Model service questions for native review:** American `For here or to go?`; British `To have here or take away?`. Longer authentic alternatives can appear after their meaning is established. Keep the same speaker identity and service setting throughout a version.

**Pronunciation:** make the drink and here/takeaway choice understandable; demonstrate polite phrasing without grading a regional accent. **Changed version:** different drink and a different service choice, with one known-language clarification. **Delayed use:** revisit the request before prices/payment in A1.06.

**Rubric:** the requested item and service choice are clear, the learner understands the confirmation and can repair a mismatch. `Could I have a coffee, please?` can achieve the same communicative goal as `I'd like a coffee, please`. If a bounded build task practises a specific taught pattern, its instruction must say so.

### Review boundary after Unit 3

Prepare a packet containing each session, target language, valid alternatives, reasons for distractors, the original and changed task, every recording and its speaker/variety, and known limitations. Include a preview exchange combining introductions and an order, but do not call three units a complete level or a validated A1 checkpoint.

Require American and British native review of their respective scripts and recordings, an English-teacher check of level/scaffolding/marking, and beginner observation of the rendered app. Track exact lesson/step/content version for issues. When a correction changes an answer or recording, recheck every dependent activity. Then author Unit 4 and the first cumulative four-unit checkpoint.

## Coverage controls before expansion

For every four-unit block, maintain a coverage sheet linking each planned capability to where it is introduced, practised with support, used independently and revisited. Tag reading/listening text types, question functions, grammar, vocabulary, interaction, pronunciation and American/British exposure. A topic appearing in a phrase card does not mark the whole capability covered.

Before calling any level complete, check the mapped descriptors against actual published material and performance tasks. Have reviewers identify omissions, overload, weak transfer and unnatural language. This map is a concrete starting architecture with review gates, not a claim that a static plan leaves no possible gap.
