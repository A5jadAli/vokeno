Prepared 18 October 2026. **Implemented locally; native, teacher and beginner approval pending.** Unit 5 completes the first phase; checkpoint 1 comes next. ## Decisions that need native and teacher scrutiny

1. **Reference variety and register.** Mexican Spanish remains the consistent speaking reference. Check natural greetings, questions, polite repair, nationality forms and contextual tú/usted. Do not judge register solely by age. Diego's Spanish origin does not make his synthetic voice evidence of a Spain accent; no Spain-accent comprehension claim is made by this pilot. Broader regional input remains planned.
2. **Naturalness in context.** Check every translation, distractor and accepted response, including `Soy estudiante`, `Hablo español e inglés`, `¿Dónde vive?`, third-person introductions and the surname/phone correction at the desk. Flag instructions that blur speaking as a character and introducing them to someone else.
3. **Numbers and spelling.** Verify each actual audio digit and letter against its transcript. Pay particular attention to 096, 7041, every ten-digit number, 15 versus 50, ñ, hache, letter-name variants and stress in dieciséis, veintidós, veintitrés and veintiséis. Confirm `veintiún años`, `treinta y un años` and exact `cien`.
4. **Names and contact details.** `Muñoz` must retain ñ in the surname field; the supplied email uses `munoz`. Check that this distinction is clear rather than contradictory. Multiple surnames are acknowledged; examples do not define a universal naming system. Phone numbers are fictional teaching examples, not numbers to call. Email examples use IANA's reserved example.com domain.
5. **Audio quality.** There are 215 newly generated AI clips, using the existing voice system. Automated checks verify inventory, decodability and plausible speech ends. They do not verify pronunciation, omitted/repeated digits, voice appropriateness, natural pacing or timing between scene turns. Listen at normal and slow speed, then test native-device playback.
6. **Load and motivation.** Ask beginners what they are trying to accomplish at each stage and what comes next. Watch the number tables and alphabet screens especially. Changed situations and optional examples support personal use, but their learning benefit must be observed rather than assumed.

### Native-speaker pass, 18 October

A line-by-line read for natural Mexican Spanish found the scripts accurate: number forms (veintiún años, treinta y un años, cien), accents, tú/usted forms, nationality agreement and the e-before-i rule are all correct. Changes made (unit numbers as they were before the split; the letter-name and desk-spelling changes now live in Unit 4):

- **Unit 1 Words** now teaches `Buenas tardes` and `Buenas noches`. The session asked what to say when arriving in the afternoon but only taught `Buenos días`; a native speaker would greet with `Buenas tardes`. The afternoon question now tests it. Two new cards; the other five keep their earned intervals.
- **Unit 2 Real talk:** the speaking model introduced Ana as `de España` two steps after a text calling her `mexicana`. The model friend is now Lucía, already established as from Spain.
- **Unit 3 Real talk:** the class-desk receptionist now uses usted (`su apellido`, `¿Su número termina en quince?`), matching the receptionist convention set in Units 1 and 2 and reinforcing the tu/su contrast just taught.
- **Unit 3 letter names:** Mexico mostly uses `be grande` / `ve chica` (RAE Ortografía 2010 notes this pair predominates in Mexico, Central America and the Andes); `uve` and `uve doble` are the Spain/RAE-recommended names. The table and audio now lead with the Mexican names and keep the others as alternatives.
- **Unit 3 hints:** `séis` is not a word on its own; hints now show the accent on the whole number (`dieciséis`, `veintiséis`).
- **Unit 3 notes:** `celular` comes first for Mexico (`móvil` in Spain), and the `años` note says why the ñ matters.
- **Unit 3 listening:** added a `sesenta`/`setenta` discrimination item, the most common tens confusion after quince/cincuenta.

Seven new clips were recorded for these lines and added to the audio sheet. Listen to them first: `Buenas tardes.`, `Buenas noches.`, `Esta es Lucía.`, `setenta`, the r–z letter list with `ve chica`, and the two receptionist questions.

Still for the human reviewers:

- **Goodbyes.** `Nos vemos` is at least as common as `Hasta luego` in Mexico. Consider adding it receptively in a later retrieval step.

**Unit 3 split (done the same day).** The first build packed numbers, age, the full alphabet, email symbols and the registration form into one unit: its Words session introduced 14 cards (the plan targets 4–7 per teaching segment) and its Real talk had 21 steps and an 11-minute estimate. It is now two units. Unit 3, "Numbers and your phone number", adds Mexican pair-reading of phone numbers (`cincuenta y cinco, doce…`) once tens are taught, the everyday `¿Me das tu número?` and a three-field details form. Unit 4, "Spell it and send it", holds the alphabet, mi/tu/su, email symbols, the desk-spelling scene and the five-field form. Eight new clips cover the new lines; listen to them as well: `¿Me das tu número?`, the two pair-read numbers, `Termina en sesenta.`, `Mi número es cincuenta y cinco…`, `Soto. ¿Así está bien?`, `ana guion bajo soto…` and `te, o, erre, erre, e, ese`. The later plan moved up by one unit (25 units, 100 sessions).

### Machine listening check, 19 October

Every one of the 135 clips was transcribed blind (the model was not given the script) with Whisper large-v3-turbo, then compared word for word with its script after normalising numbers and letter names. Clips that differed were split at their pauses and each piece transcribed on its own. The audio sheet's status column now reads "Machine-checked".

- 124 clips matched their script on the first pass. Seven more matched once written forms were normalised (`21 años` for `veintiún años`, capital letters for spoken letter names, `@` for `arroba`).
- Three were defective and were re-recorded: `Hola, me llamo Sara. Soy de Pakistán.` stopped after the first sentence; the spelled `eme, u, eñe, o, zeta` had a `u` that sounded like `o`; `Este es Diego.` was heard as `Dievo`. The new takes transcribe correctly, piece by piece.
- Acoustics: no clipping and no unexpected pauses. Loudness ranges from about −27 to −18 LUFS. Ana's voice sits at the soft end throughout, so in the desk scenes the receptionist is noticeably louder than her replies. Normalising loudness across the whole clip library is a follow-up for all languages, not a defect of these units.

This check confirms that each clip says its script. It does not judge accent or naturalness; a native listener should still spot-check a sample, starting with the 15 clips added on 18 October.

## Feedback and evidence boundaries

- New dictation steps hide the transcript, including accessible play labels, until the learner requests **Read instead** or completes the exercise. Slow playback remains available. Text help records the objective item as assisted, including after a reload; it does not block completion.
- The form preserves correct entries, marks incorrect fields individually, focuses the first field to fix and keeps drafts across leaving/reloading. A wrong phone number can be fixed without retyping the name or email. Each form (three fields in Unit 3, five in Unit 4) contributes one objective item, not separate official marks.
- Exact detail grading keeps leading zeros, accents/ñ in names, and email punctuation. Phone grouping spaces, parentheses and hyphens are accepted. Names ignore case and normalise composed Unicode; email domain case is ignored while the local part stays exact. New explicit spelling exercises require the taught spelling. General phrase exercises retain the app's existing accent feedback policy.
- Speaking models and changed situations remain optional. Speaking is unrecorded self-practice. A learner tapping **I said it aloud** is not pronunciation, fluency or oral-exam evidence.
- The Unit 4 registration is a five-field precursor with English support and unlimited practice. It is not the complete DELE W1 task or a timed/mock exam. Full writing, interaction, cumulative checkpoints and calibrated readiness evidence remain planned.
