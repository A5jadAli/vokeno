# English M3 participant sessions

This is a facilitator guide for the three-unit pilot, not a proficiency test. The tasks and scales below are our pilot instruments. They have not been calibrated or validated as a CEFR assessment. No human participant results have been recorded yet.

## Prepare

Use the actual pilot build on the participant's own phone. Note the build, lesson versions, operating system, first language, starting English ability and chosen English model. Use a participant code such as P01 rather than a name. Explain that you are testing the course, that help and breaks are available, and that they can stop. Obtain agreement before recording a voice or screen. Keep identifiable recordings and findings outside the repository; `artifacts/` is ignored for local work.

Invite both people who cannot yet hold an English exchange and people who know some words or short phrases. Include people interested in everyday life and work. Begin with a small qualitative round, for example three from each starting group, and recruit further if important differences remain unexplored. Six participants is a practical first round, not a statistical efficacy sample or proof that all beginners can use the course. Include different phone sizes and accessibility settings where possible.

An American reviewer and a British reviewer should each check their own models and cross-variety comprehension. An English teacher should separately check teaching order, task demands, valid alternatives and feedback. Native-speaker status alone does not establish teaching or assessment expertise. Reviewer availability has been confirmed by the product owner; completed reviews remain pending.

Instructions are currently in English. Before the session, prepare a reviewed spoken explanation of the task purpose in the participant's first language when needed. Do not translate the target English response or provide a model during the first independent attempt. Record instructional translation separately from help producing or understanding the target language. Do not count inability to understand the interface as inability to communicate in English.

## Navigation observation

Give one task at a time. Do not point at the button first. Let the participant describe what they think will happen. If they are stuck, record the problem and help them continue. Do not use a fixed time limit as a pass/fail rule.

1. “You want to start learning English here. Show me where you would begin.” Record first choice, confusion and whether any help was needed.
2. “Listen to these people. Listen again if you want.” Observe play, replay and meaning support. Check the device voice or recording actually plays.
3. After an incorrect answer, ask “What would you do next?” Observe whether feedback helps a successful retry. Do not deliberately tell the learner to give a wrong answer if they are already struggling.
4. Leave partway through a session and reopen the app. Observe whether the learner can resume and knows what is saved.
5. After a session, ask “What can you do now, and what would you do next?” Accept a break as an appropriate choice. Optional practice must not feel compulsory.
6. After Unit 3, ask “What is available next? Does this mean you have finished all A1 English?” Check that review and calls are findable and that the partial-course scope is understood.

Spread the units across comfortable sessions. Do not make a novice complete all twelve sessions in one sitting merely to match the automated browser test.

## Changed-task protocol

After each unit, use one task form below without showing the app's model. A cooperative partner speaks clearly and waits. A request for repetition is successful communication, not a failure. Use a fictional profile or the learner's own details, at their choice. Do not require the exact wording in the expected evidence column.

At a later visit, ideally about a week later, use the other form. Alternate A-first and B-first across participants where practical. Record the actual gap and any intervening practice. If language support or a model is needed, provide it and record it; label that attempt supported. Do not call a changed detail in a familiar task independent transfer to all real-life situations.

| ---- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| 1    | Meet a partner called Ella. Give the supplied name Ben or your own name. Ask the partner's name. The partner asks you to say your name again. Close the exchange.                          | Meet Alex. Give the supplied name Ella or your own name. Ask the name. The partner asks for repetition. Close.                           | Greeting, understandable name, name question, response to repetition and a closing. Hi/Hello, I'm/I am/My name is, You too/Nice to meet you too, Bye/Goodbye all work. A clear name alone can answer a name question. Do not demand a fully expanded sentence.                                   |
| 2    | Read a profile: Name Alex; From Boston; Home London; Work/study student. Introduce the person as yourself. Partner checks “You live in Boston?” Correct it and ask the partner a question. | Profile: Name Ella; From London; Home Boston; Work/study shop/store. Partner checks “You live in London?” Correct it and ask a question. | Origin and current home stay distinct. Clear correction and a relevant question. “No, London” can correct the home. For here/To have here, To go/To take away/Takeaway please are valid when they convey the chosen place. No prices, payment, cup sizes or customisation. |

Provide the pictures, profile labels and city/name spellings, so the task checks communication rather than guessing or spelling unseen vocabulary. The participant can use a familiar city in a subsequent optional personal attempt; do not treat difficulty pronouncing a new city as failure of the taught pattern.

## Sample all four skills

Keep these short and tied to the same unit. Collect the actual response or an accurate observation, not only a score.

- Unit 1 reading/writing: read a name badge; write an introduction using that name. Listening: identify the repeated name in a short exchange. Speaking: the changed introduction above.
- Unit 2 reading/writing: read the supplied profile; write a short introduction keeping origin and home distinct. Listening: a partner gives the two place facts in a different order; the learner identifies the current home. Speaking: the correction and question above.
- Unit 3 reading/writing: read the pictured order; write a short drink-and-location note. Listening: identify the incorrect drink in a confirmation. Speaking: order and repair above.

Rotate clear American and British input using already taught language. Identify the actual voice/model used. Allow transcripts for learning but record them as listening support. Do not compare unlike tasks as if their scores were equivalent.

## Record evidence

Use [the observation sheet](english-m3-observations.csv), one row per task attempt and skill. Keep separate rows for the first attempt, a supported retry and the delayed attempt. The sheet is deliberately empty; it is not simulated evidence.

For each observed dimension, record 0, 1, 2 or `not observed`:

| Dimension              | 0                                                 | 1                                                                    | 2                                                                |
| ---------------------- | ------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Task outcome           | Required meaning was not conveyed/understood      | Some required information was conveyed; a key detail remains unclear | The task's required meaning and details were conveyed/understood |
| Spoken intelligibility | Partner could not recover the needed meaning      | Partner recovered it after clarification                             | Partner understood the relevant words; an accent is acceptable   |
| Interaction and repair | Could not respond to or repair the changed detail | Responded or repaired partially                                      | Resolved the change and continued/closed appropriately           |
| Language for this task | Forms repeatedly prevented the needed meaning     | Meaning mostly clear despite errors or limited range                 | Taught forms or valid alternatives served the task clearly       |

Use `not observed` for dimensions the task does not sample. Record support independently: none, repetition, transcript, model, translation of instructions, translated target language, partner hint, or facilitator help. A score of 2 after a model is supported success, not independent performance. Do not average these small-task ratings into a CEFR level.

Ask “How comfortable did this feel?” before and after, using 1 (not comfortable) to 5 (very comfortable), with an optional explanation. Confidence is self-report. Keep it separate from task performance, completion and whether the learner enjoyed the activity. Record when repetition was requested successfully, not only errors.

## Live coaching and phone checks

Try all three calls in both models using the selected pilot client and updated backend. Test a normal reply, a valid alternative, a request for repetition, a short reply, a meaning-changing error, silence and a changed detail. Also try choosing Meetings or IELTS as a beginner; the call must remain within the unit. Record the actual transcript and listen to the audio before judging delivery.

Check microphone denial, retry, mute, leaving during permission, interruption, hangup, app backgrounding and network loss. Verify that a stopped call stops using the microphone. Verify the after-call card does not invent errors, accent judgements or personal facts. A generated transcript is not evidence of pronunciation. Synthetic text-input calls cover only part of this matrix.

On at least one real Android phone and one iPhone, check recorded audio, per-line speaker variety, slow playback, replay, cached audio offline, and an uncached fallback. Try missing regional voices, larger text, screen-reader labels and keyboard visibility. Browser emulation does not replace these device checks.

## Decide what changes

Log each finding with the lesson version, step or audio hash, observed behaviour, expected behaviour, severity, proposed correction, owner and re-review status. Use the [review page](english-m3-review.html) for script/audio decisions. Keep participant observations separate from editorial sign-off. Do not fill pending rows with the author's or an AI's opinion as if a participant had supplied them.

Resolve blockers in meaning, marking, sound and navigation before expansion. Re-review the exact changed version and listen again to replaced audio. Major instructional problems also need resolution or an explicit, documented pilot limitation. If participants need translated instructions, add and review the selected support-language pack before claiming unaided access for that group. Do not mark M3 complete until human reviews, beginner observation, later changed-task evidence and real-device/live-call checks are recorded.
